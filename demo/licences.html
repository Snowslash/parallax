About & licences

Parallax is a projection-geometry teaching lab using simplified models. Not for clinical use. The models are not anatomically validated and the lessons have not been tested with learners.

The rig does not model machine travel or collisions. One view, or a pair that looks reassuring, is not a general test of containment.

Responses reset when you refresh. Only your colour-theme preference is saved. There are no accounts, uploads or analytics.

How the X-ray images are made

Each image is calculated through the 3D model, not taken as a screenshot of it. For every pixel, Parallax traces a straight line from the X-ray source to the detector and measures how far it passes through bone or wire. A longer path through material, or material that blocks more of the beam, makes that pixel brighter. Structures at different depths can overlap in one view and separate in another.

The femur’s outer cortex is set to block more of the beam than its inner core; the wire blocks it more strongly still. The Uniform option treats the whole bone as one material. All views use the same brightness scale, without automatically adjusting the contrast of each image.

3D Explore and both femur pages calculate new projections in your browser when you move the beam. The guided projection-fundamentals lesson uses images calculated in advance. Looking around the 3D scene only changes your viewpoint; it does not change an acquired image.

The images leave out soft tissue, scatter, the range of X-ray energies, blur and detector noise. Pixel edges can shimmer. These simplifications help show the geometry, but do not reproduce all the features of a clinical X-ray.

Femur lesson

The wire represents a planned screw, with its tip standing in for the screw tip. The exercise uses fixed wire positions and AP and lateral views chosen for this model. These views and measurements still need expert review; the lateral view makes the neck look shorter.

The tip-to-marker distance is not clinical TAD. It measures to the same gold surface marker in both images and adjusts for magnification using the known depth of each endpoint. The dimensions are a teaching scale, not calibrated anatomy. The 25 mm combined-distance limit and 8 mm centring limit are rules for this exercise, not clinical grading rules.

Baumgaertner et al., 1995 studied lag-screw tip–apex distance and cutout in sliding hip-screw fixation. Its 25 mm finding is a teaching reference, not a guarantee of safe fixation. The indexed abstract was inspected, not the complete paper. This lesson does not assess fracture reduction, implant selection, stability or overall fixation suitability.

Models

The cylinder and wire are mathematical shapes. Femur uses the whole BodyParts3D left-femur surface with an authored inner core, not a recovered tissue segmentation. Its two image models use the same view and fixed intensity mapping. It remains experimental.
